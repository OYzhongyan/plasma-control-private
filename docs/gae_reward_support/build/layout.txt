               Reward Support Across Episode Positions:
          Exact Counts and Their Effect on GAE Critic Targets
                     Technical analysis for the tokamak control experiments

                                            October 26, 2026


                                                 Abstract
          A fixed-length rollout generally ends at a different position in each episode. Consequently,
      advantages associated with different episode positions use different numbers of observed rewards.
      We derive the exact cumulative support count Ct , its periodic structure, and its relation to
      critic-target error. For the example M = 500, N = 256, and m = 64, the first position uses
      130 rewards per target on average, whereas the final position uses one. Under a controlled
      endpoint-error perturbation, their average sensitivity coeﬀicients are approximately 0.0651 and
      0.995, respectively. All results assume correct bootstrapping at artificial episode time
      limits. The analysis separates reward support, sample count, target bias, and state coverage so
      that the effect can be quantified without treating these different quantities as interchangeable.


1    Sampling convention and the quantities being counted
The sampling convention is the one illustrated by Figure 14.1 of the accompanying Tokamak
Plasma Simulator Guide: rollout windows continue across resets, while GAE recursion stops at
both rollout boundaries and episode boundaries. The derivation here is self-contained and does
not require that figure.
Consider one environment. It produces m episodes of exactly M action steps each, with no early
failures. Rollouts contain N transitions. The first rollout starts at the first transition of the first
episode. Episode j is indexed by j = 1, . . . , m, and position within an episode by t = 1, . . . , M . At
position t, the environment starts in sj,t , takes aj,t , receives rj,t , and reaches sj,t+1 .
An artificial time limit resets the environment after transition M but does not make the underlying
control task terminal. Its final state is used for bootstrap; rewards from the reset episode are never
appended to the old episode’s GAE. A final partial rollout is assumed to be processed, or sampling
is continued until the containing rollout is complete. Discarding an incomplete final rollout would
require excluding the discarded samples from the counts below.

7.2   Why extending episodes can improve endpoint values

Consider a deterministic chain s1 → · · · → sM → sM +1 , where training always resets at sM +1 .
Correct bootstrap retains YM = rM + γVold (sM +1 ), but there is no observed outgoing transition
from sM +1 in this dataset. If all observed Bellman equations are satisfied and the boundary value
has error c, subtracting the true Bellman equations gives

                                      e(st ) = γe(st+1 ) = γ M −t+1 c .                         (7.3)

Thus small Bellman residuals on the observed chain do not determine the unknown boundary value.
Extending episodes makes previously unobserved outgoing transitions available and moves the
unconstrained boundary farther away. States already covered elsewhere can share these constraints
through learning; what matters is state-transition coverage, not the timestep label by itself.
A rollout boundary is different: the next rollout normally resumes the same episode, so the outgoing
data are eventually collected. It limits the current GAE fragment without necessarily creating a
persistent coverage gap. An episode reset removes the continuation unless other trajectories visit
the relevant states.


8     Implications for the controlled experiment
The direct, quantifiable effect is:

      Late-episode targets have less observed reward support and greater sensitivity to their
      final value prediction. Correct bootstrap supplies that prediction but does not supply
      the missing realized rewards. Longer episodes can both extend some fragments and
      train the predictions on newly observed continuation states.

The following measurements separate these mechanisms:

  1. Record Kj,t , Ct , and pt (k) by episode position, together with β̄t . These are sampling-
     geometry quantities, not critic-error estimates.

  2. Freeze a policy, critic, and normalization statistics; use common long trajectories to compare
     targets constructed with different rollout/reset cuts. Estimate reference values from indepen-
     dent continuations where feasible. This separates estimator differences from policy changes
     during training.

  3. Measure target error and critic error by position, as well as current, intermediate, and end-
     point prediction errors. A larger β̄t matters most where endpoint errors are substantial or
     systematically signed.

  4. Compare training runs with the same physical timestep, reference prefix, reward definition,
     total interaction budget, and correct timeout bootstrap. Longer episodes change the state
     distribution: the first H steps occupy a fraction H/M of samples when H ≤ M , which should
     be reported.

At fixed rollout length, increasing M does not remove rollout boundaries. For example, in the first
episode with N = 256, position 500 has support 1 when M = 500, but support 13 when M ≥ 512,
because the next rollout boundary is 512. Further extension can improve learned continuation
values even when it does not increase that particular sample’s immediate support.
For E synchronized environments with identical episode lengths and phases, all environments have
the same Kj,t . Pooling m episodes per environment multiplies Ct by E but does not change K̄t
or β̄t . Variable episode lengths and early failures require the actual cumulative start index in
Equation (2.3); the fixed-length periodic formulas no longer apply.
This document assumes the timeout semantics described in [2]: artificial episode boundaries retain
final-state bootstrap, whereas genuine terminal states have zero continuation value. It makes
no claim that a particular backend currently enforces these semantics; they are the stipulated
conditions of this analysis. All numerical tables use M = 500 as a mathematical example, not as
a measurement of the actual simulator horizon.

    if t in [1, 100, 256, 257, 400, 450, 499, 500]:
        print(t, sum(K), sum(K)/m, measured)
